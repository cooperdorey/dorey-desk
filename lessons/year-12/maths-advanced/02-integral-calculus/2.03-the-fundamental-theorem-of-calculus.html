FactEpisode 3 · The Fundamental Theorem
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
$\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=[F(x)]_{a}^{b}=F(b)-F(a)$
$F(x)$ is any primitive of $f(x)$, and $f$ is continuous from $a$ to $b$.
The $+C$ is not needed. It cancels: $(F(b)+C)-(F(a)+C)=F(b)-F(a)$.
Integrating gives signed area, so we can find areas without any geometry.

FactEpisode 3 · The Fundamental Theorem
Adding and reversing limits
$\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f\,dx+\int_{b}^{c}f\,dx=\int_{a}^{c}f\,dx\qquad\int_{a}^{b}f\,dx=-\int_{b}^{a}f\,dx$
$f$ is continuous from $a$ to $c$. The first rule joins neighbouring areas. The second comes from $F(b)-F(a)=-(F(a)-F(b))$.
Swapping the limits changes the sign.

FactEpisode 3 · The Fundamental Theorem
The area function $A(x)$
$A(x)=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{x}f(t)\,dt\ \Longrightarrow\ A'(x)=f(x)$
$A(x)$ is the signed area from a fixed point $a$ up to $x$. As $x$ increases by a small step $h$, the area grows by about $f(x)\times h$. So $A$ is a primitive of $f$.
The variable inside the integral is a dummy: $t$ is used so it is not confused with the upper limit $x$.

We Do 9Episode 3 · The Fundamental Theorem
The graph of $y=f(x)$ is shown. The point $Q$ at $x=0$ is a horizontal point of inflection, and the graph has a maximum at $x=1$ and a minimum at $x=3$. Let $A(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt$.
How many points of inflection does the graph $y=A(x)$ have?
A. $1$
B. $2$
C. $3$
D. $4$
$A'(x)=f(x)$, so $A''(x)=f'(x)$.
Inflections of $A$ occur where $f'$ changes sign, that is, at the maximum and minimum of $f$: $x=1$ and $x=3$.
At $x=0$, $f'=0$ but does not change sign, so it gives no inflection of $A$.
The answer is B.
